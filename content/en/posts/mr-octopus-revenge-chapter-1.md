It was 10:47 p.m. when Zhang Yu'an sent the last of her money away.

One hundred eighty-six thousand yuan.

The payee note read two characters: A-Zhe.

She stared at them for a while. The screen dimmed on its own; she woke it, entered the password, pressed her thumb to the sensor. Transfer complete. The system chime was soft, one small ding, like a needle sliding into her ear and disappearing.

That was all of her savings. This month's pay, the part of her housing fund she could withdraw, and thirty thousand borrowed from her college roommate. She had done the math. It was enough to cover the "hole" Cheng Zhe had described.

On the phone, Cheng Zhe said he had been cheated. The project was dead, the money he had fronted was gone, and the other side had promised to turn up at his office if he didn't clear the debt within three days. His voice was hoarse, close to breaking. Yu'an, I have nowhere left to turn. You're the only one I can ask.

She believed him.

Three years. From college to that last year, she had believed him. He remembered that she hated cilantro. He remembered the cramps that left her in a cold sweat. He remembered her first payday, when he ordered a cake with "Ms. Zhang Yu'an, the future is yours" written on the candles.

A man like that did not cheat people. Not her.

She put the phone down and went to wash her face. In the mirror, the woman looking back had bruised shadows under her eyes and hair so oily it lay flat against her scalp. How many extra shifts had she worked this past half month for that "hole"? She had lost count.

Her phone buzzed on the sink. A red dot: Moments.

She hadn't meant to open it. Her thumb moved on its own.

And then she just stood there, half her face still covered in cleanser, staring at the post.

Cheng Zhe's. Forty minutes ago. Nine photos. The first was a close-up of a wristwatch, its dial giving off a faint blue shine under a spotlight. She knew the brand. Two hundred thousand yuan at the counter, easy. The rest were a private room, foreign liquor, cigars, and a few women she didn't recognize, laughing close, shoulders pressed against Cheng Zhe's.

The caption was one line:

【Brothers, that chapter's closed. Nobody goes home sober tonight.】

That chapter's closed.

Zhang Yu'an heard her own heartbeat. One beat. Then another. Slow and heavy, like someone drumming inside her chest.

She didn't cry.

She turned the phone face down on the sink and walked back through it, one item at a time. Three days ago, he had nowhere to turn. Two days ago, the other side was pressing him. Yesterday, wait a little longer, the money would come back to her the moment it arrived. Today, he wore a two-hundred-thousand-yuan watch and closed the chapter.

The nights she had stayed up, the money she had borrowed, the face she had put on when she asked her roommate — to him, that chapter was closed.

She thought of last month, when her best friend Lin Weiwei had told her: "Yu'an, that guy A-Zhe doesn't sit right with me. Keep your guard up."

She had defended him then.

Now she remembered. While Lin Weiwei was warning her, her eyes had drifted. Just for a second.

* * *

At one in the morning, Zhang Yu'an did not go home.

She went back to the office.

Her access card still worked. She hadn't quit. Data annotation was tiring, tedious work, but the server room stayed open around the clock and late shifts came with taxi reimbursement. She needed that reimbursement. Right now she needed money very badly.

The tower was empty. The sound-activated lights came on one by one ahead of her and went out one by one behind.

Data annotation sat on the seventeenth floor. She sat down, opened her computer, and began boxing, tagging, submitting, the motions mechanical. Screen light on her face. Her expression calm, calm as though nothing had happened.

Only she knew the drum in her chest was still beating.

At two, with the queue cleared, she got up to fetch water.

Passing the server room, she stopped.

It sat at the end of the corridor. Behind glass doors, rows of server racks, indicator lights blinking on and off, fans humming low like a herd of sleeping animals.

Behind the last row of racks was the company's abandoned lab area. Colleagues had told her about it: the leftovers of a project that died three years ago, still stacked in there. Nobody dealt with it. Nobody dared touch it.

Then—

"Beep."

A soft electronic note, from deep inside the lab area.

Zhang Yu'an stood still. Her fingers tightened around the paper cup.

An abandoned server room at two in the morning should not make a sound.

The beep came again, then a second, a third, steady, like a device idling on standby, or like—

Like something opening its eyes.
